A client once came to me with a clear brief: design a two-day leadership programme for their middle managers. Budget approved, dates pencilled, room booked.

Three conversations in, it was obvious that their managers already knew how to lead. What they didn't have was authority to make decisions without three levels of sign-off. No workshop was going to fix that. A workshop would have made it worse — you cannot teach people to be decisive and then send them back into a system that punishes decisiveness.

## The diagnosis is the work

The instinct in learning and development is to answer the question you're asked. Someone requests a programme; you design a programme. It's a reasonable instinct, and it produces an enormous amount of well-designed, well-facilitated training that changes nothing.

Before designing anything I want to understand five things:

1. What is the organisation trying to achieve?
2. What needs to be different for that to happen?
3. What capabilities are currently missing?
4. What will leaders need to think, do or lead differently?
5. What will make the change stick?

Question three is where most briefs come apart. "Missing capability" is the assumed diagnosis, and often it isn't the real one. The capability is there. What's missing is permission, or clarity, or a structure that lets people use what they already have.

## Saying so is the job

Telling a client that their budgeted, scheduled programme is not the right intervention is uncomfortable. It also builds more trust than any amount of good facilitation, because it demonstrates that you are working on their problem rather than selling your product.

In that particular case we ran a much smaller piece of work — two sessions with the executive team about decision rights, and a short set of guidelines about what managers could sign off alone. It cost a fraction of the programme. It was also the only version of the work that would have made any difference.

Sometimes the answer is a leadership programme. Sometimes it's an executive learning journey. Sometimes it's coaching. And sometimes the most valuable thing in the room is the person willing to say that none of those is what you need.
